A workshop you can return to

When someone opens a lab page, they need to work out where to begin, what to try and how to keep what they have made. I built the platform around that sequence. Each Friday has a check-in, an experiment and a save-and-reflect step. A student can open a guide when it becomes useful and then return to their own project.

The map keeps all eight labs open. Missing a Friday does not lock someone out of the next activity. Earlier exercises remain available when a later project needs a particular skill. The page also offers a practical starting point for someone who is new or returning after an absence.

The material includes editable code exercises, playable demonstrations, Construct guidance, a research library, computing-history activities and publishing help. The important connection is between trying something, observing what it does and making the next decision.

Open a project→Try one change→Test it→Save and reflect

Code you can investigate

The web exercises let students work with HTML, CSS and JavaScript directly. A change in a CSS value can be compared with the rendered result. A JavaScript action can be tested against a prediction. The colour studio and code explanations make the relationship between a rule and its effect available for inspection.

Construct offers another way to approach the same questions. What object receives an action? Which condition must be true? What does the system remember when someone enters another room? The toolkit gathers the relevant editor actions, shortcuts, troubleshooting and export routes, including links to the course's existing templates and guides.

The Gate Engine makes rules and transitions concrete. A student can use that experience to think about their own work: whether a door responds to a key, a remembered decision, a delay or a different condition. The point of an example is to make a rule understandable enough to change deliberately.

Learning to judge the output

The AI-assisted practice raises a further question: how do you assess a proposed solution when it looks convincing? A useful test is specific. Follow the link. Activate the button. Read the code that changes the state. Check the source behind a claim. Replace a generic description with the actual material or decision.

The new coding museum gives this topic more room, alongside practical VS Code advice. It covers project folders, finding files, formatting, debugging, reusable CSS, animation and performance. Students can change one specimen and compare the roles of HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

Practice and assessment have different rules. Lab 2 includes AI-assisted coding practice. The current final-assignment guide prohibits AI in making the assessed work and in writing the accompanying text. The platform directs students to that distinction and to the official brief and Mitt UiB.

Making and research belong together

The academic library begins with the student's own object: what are you making, and what might help you understand it? Its genre explorer connects forms of digital work with examples and research directions. A student can move from an interaction to a concept, a work to study or a search term.

The library also includes starting points for books and articles, searches through Google Scholar and Sikt NVA, source evaluation and Chicago author-date guidance. These resources support the move from finding something that sounds relevant to checking what it actually contributes.

That connection matters for the final assessment. The creative work and the accompanying discussion need to speak to one another. A useful process note records an intention, a choice, a test and an observation. It gives the student something particular to examine when discussing genre, theme, form and audience.

The platform's final guide brings the creative work, written discussion, walkthrough and working link into view. Its publishing guidance helps students check the version another person can access. Official submission requirements and deadlines remain with the course brief, Mitt UiB and Inspera.

Access includes being able to find your place

The accessibility console offers text-size controls, a high-contrast mode, a calmer layout and reduced motion. It also responds to the device's reduced-motion preference. Those options sit alongside ordinary interaction choices such as headings, labels, keyboard routes and visible feedback.

The repeated three-step structure helps a student understand where they are within a session. Morning and afternoon clock choices display the same 105-minute plan with the appropriate times. The scheduled breaks remain part of the plan.

Progress is personal. Three stars per lab make 24 across the eight sessions, with local checklists and notes when browser storage is available. These are a record on that device, not a grade, an attendance register or formal approval of obligatory work. Saving a checklist does not save the actual project files; the finishing step asks students to keep those separately.

Storage behaviour is explained where it matters. A browser can refuse storage, a different device can have a different record, and clearing data can remove local progress. The teaching material remains open even when that personal record is unavailable.

Today's tools have a history

The time machine approaches computing history through small actions: ordered cards, a read-and-write tape, a relay to inspect, a musical sequence, linked information and a connected web. The historical account and the fantasy laboratory are identified separately.

The coding museum uses the existing Lovelace punched-card and Turing tape-machine exhibits. Their imagined conversations concern practical questions: what a program changes, how to investigate a failure, and how to keep a visual language consistent.

The dialogue is labelled as fiction. Historical links lead to Lovelace’s notes and the Turing Digital Archive. A student can read the transcript or use the same motion and accessibility controls as the other lab pages.

The next lab: make a future someone can try

For 6 November, the Future lab asks students to begin with something they care about and turn it into one action with a consequence. A future can be hopeful, ordinary, funny or difficult. It does not have to begin with a dystopian city or a large fictional world.

Three optional prompts invite students to think about care, access or memory. They answer with their own situation and decide which interaction to build. An existing project or a different idea is equally welcome.

The session then moves through choosing a future, sketching an interaction and keeping a manageable scope. After the first break, the task is to build a first version. After the second, a neighbour tries it before the maker explains the intended result.

That test has three simple questions: What did you think you could do? What changed? What future did you think this was? The maker records one observation and one next step for the feedback clinic. Someone whose code is not running can still bring a paper version or a well-described blocker to the discussion.

The optional coding museum is available throughout. A student can use the CSS section when a layout breaks or the VS Code section when navigating files becomes the obstacle. There is no expectation to read the entire guide during Friday's session.
