OPTIONAL EXPERIMENT / DESIGN & DIGITAL ETHICS

The digital wheelchair ramp

A staircase assumes that everyone can use stairs. An interface can make similar assumptions about sight, movement, attention or how quickly someone works. Who gets to participate when we build it this way?

In this lab, the ramp is our analogy for removing barriers. The accessibility bar above is a working example you can inspect and question. A toolbar alone does not make a website accessible: readable defaults, meaningful HTML, keyboard access and testing still matter.

A connection to DIKULT106

DIKULT106, Critical Approaches to Technology and Society, examines technology through cultural, social and normative questions. Its official description lists accessibility and digital ethics among topics that may vary by year. Our question here is: whose needs become the default, and who has to ask for an exception?

Your experiment: remove one barrier

Take about 10 minutes, or use this during your own-project time. No extra submission or points are required.

  1. Try the keyboard. Use Tab and Shift+Tab to move between controls. Look for a visible focus outline. Reach the button below, then use Enter or Space.
  2. Change the reading conditions. Open the accessibility bar. Try 200% text, high contrast and calm layout. Can you still find and operate every control? Try browser zoom too.
  3. Choose your motion. Turn on reduced motion. On the lab map, decorative movement and animated transitions should stop. Your device preference takes priority.
  4. Test your own project. Find one barrier, make one change, and test again. For a Construct project, consider an alternative to a pointer-only action, readable instructions or a way to pause movement.
  5. Explain the decision. Write: ?The barrier was __. I changed __. This helps __. I still need to test __.? Ask someone to try it without telling them where to click.

The gate is ready. Try reaching the button with Tab.

Test the interface, rather than pretending to experience someone else's disability. People have different access needs; offer choices and listen to feedback.

Build the ramp into your code

A native button already supports keyboard activation. Use a button for an action and a link for navigation. Do not replace either with a clickable decorative box.

<button type="button" id="gate">Open gate</button>
<p id="result" role="status">Ready</p>
<script>
document.querySelector('#gate').addEventListener('click', () => {
  document.querySelector('#result').textContent = 'Gate opened';
});
</script>

Add a clear keyboard focus indicator and respect reduced motion in your CSS. JavaScript animations also need to check the device preference.

button:focus-visible, a:focus-visible {
  outline: 3px solid #1759b0;
  outline-offset: 4px;
}
@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  .decoration { animation: none; }
}

The contrast of your focus colour depends on its background. Test your actual design rather than copying a colour without checking it.

Inspect our working files: toolbar JavaScript and toolbar CSS. Reading preferences stay in this browser where storage is available; they are not sent to Ingrid. Resetting them leaves your lab notes and progress intact.

What counts as a useful test?

  • W3C: keyboard access ? can you operate the task without a pointer?
  • W3C: resize text ? can text reach 200% without losing content or functionality?
  • W3C: reflow ? does content adapt to a narrow reading area?
  • W3C: text contrast ? are the words distinguishable from their background?
  • W3C: interaction animation ? can unnecessary movement be disabled?

These are starting points, not a claim that this site meets every WCAG requirement. If a quest gate or any other part of the lab prevents you from taking part, tell Ingrid so we can find a workable way through together.
