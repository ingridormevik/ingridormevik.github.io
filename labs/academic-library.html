A BOOK HAS ITS OWN MAP

Find your theme. Follow an author.

AT THE FRONT
The contents show the chapters.

Look for a chapter connected to your work or theme. Use it to get your bearings, then read the relevant argument in context.

USUALLY AT THE BACK
The index points a theme to pages.

Look up a word such as agency, identity or play. Follow its page numbers. Try related terms if your exact word is missing. Not every book has an index.

Read around the passage. Finding a word is the beginning of understanding what the author means.

AT THE END, OR AFTER A CHAPTER
The bibliography points to other sources.

Recognise an author, or spot a title connected to your theme? Take the full reference and search for that title in Oria or Google Scholar.

Follow it to the original work and read it before citing it as a source you have read. Use the bibliography as a lead to your next source.

Try this with the source

Find a passage that helps you see your work differently. What is the author arguing, and what evidence or examples do they use?

START HERE / WHAT RESEARCH DO I NEED?

You do not need to know your genre yet.

Describe what your project does. Choose what sounds like your work. Then answer any questions you can. We will suggest what to search for and why. Choose “not sure” whenever you need to. There are no right answers or points.

A project can be a game, electronic literature and an artwork at the same time. A choice button does not automatically make something a game. Text on a screen does not automatically make it electronic literature. Use the result as a starting point to investigate.

Start with what you are making.

Choose as many as you need. These are everyday starting points, not final genre labels.

01 / What can someone do in your project?

For example: choose a reply, open a door, read a page or just watch.

02 / Does everyone get the story in the same order?

A story is nonlinear when people can take different routes through it.

03 / Is there something to play or try?

Rules might let people solve a puzzle, collect something, move or change the world.

04 / Are the words part of the artwork?

A poem is different from a button label. Both use words, but the words do different jobs.

05 / What else matters in the experience?

Choose the closest answer. The first question also lets you choose several kinds of work.

06 / What do you care about in this project?

Your interests can guide your research. Think of your life, job, community or other courses.

DIKULT105 / FROM IDEA TO SOMETHING PEOPLE CAN USE

Make it. Try it. Understand it.

Choose a route from the collection. Build one small part, find research that helps you question it, and use what you discover to improve it. You can return to any stage.

What will I learn, and where do I practise it?
  1. 01

    Choose an experience

    Who is it for? What can that person do? Start with one screen, scene or task.

    Find a making route
  2. 02

    Make it respond

    Connect an input to a visible or audible result. Learn the code or design tool through a working example.

    A first JavaScript interactionBuild and play in Construct
  3. 03

    Shape the experience

    Use conventions for your form. Compare how type, colour, sound, pacing or movement changes its meaning.

    Try the aesthetics bench
  4. 04

    Investigate a choice

    Use a relevant source to question your design. Examine the tools and industries behind it: ownership, labour, access or representation.

    Find and read a source
  5. 05

    Test and work together

    Watch someone try a real task. Revise what gets in the way. In a group, agree responsibilities and negotiate changes.

    Use the Test LabCheck access and alternatives
  6. 06

    Share and explain

    Export a working artefact. Use examples, sources and test evidence to discuss your choices, limitations and contribution in a critical report.

    Export and publishRead and cite accurately

This practice route connects to UiB's official DIKULT105 learning outcomes. These are lab suggestions. Your assignment brief and course reading list are in MittUiB.

Can a podcast, film or useful app belong here too?

Yes, these forms belong in digital culture research. Whether one meets your assignment depends on the brief. Ask what your digital design contributes: a listener can choose a route, a viewer can compare accounts, or an app can help someone participate.

A button alone does not make a work a game or electronic literature. Explain what the interaction does for the experience. You can prototype the idea with the web and Construct skills from the labs; some forms need other tools.

The collection mixes genres, media, techniques and making practices, and labels which is which. Accessibility, aesthetics, ethics and collaboration matter across the collection. You do not need to try every route.

MIX YOUR OWN ROUTE

A genre label is a starting point, not a box.

A Pride Park project might be an exploration game about belonging. If reading and digital language are central, e-lit research may also help. If it unfolds across a game, social posts and a physical event, investigate transmedia storytelling too.

Say what is actually present in the work. A topic you care about does not, by itself, decide the genre.

Four questions. Four kinds of label.
QuestionExamples
What form does it take?Game, poem, documentary, comic, podcast.
How does it work?Branching paths, generated text, simulation, live participation.
What is its tone or story tradition?Horror, romance, comedy, fantasy, science fiction, memoir.
What do you want to investigate?Belonging, climate, gender, memory, power, accessibility.

For games, narrow the search when useful: action, adventure, role-playing, strategy, simulation, puzzle, rhythm, sports or racing. These can overlap too. A horror story might use a puzzle game, a podcast or a visual novel.

Build a search: one form + one question. Try exploration games belonging, podcast identity or digital poetry memory. Start broad, then follow terms you find in scholarship.

Two ideas for your textEffort and actionConcepts that help you explain what your work is, not only how it looks.Open the research route

THE RESEARCH DESK / FOLLOW YOUR CURIOSITY

New to research? Open the step-by-step screenshot guide for Scholar, NVA, UiB access, Internet Archive and Tibi →

Google your idea. Try unfamiliar terms, follow an author's name and look at what an article cites. You do not need to know the perfect search words before you begin.

Find the words

Try two or three terms: game interfaces, podcast identity or accessible app design. Too few useful results? Drop a term or try a synonym. Put an exact article title in quotation marks.

In Google Scholar, try Related articles and Cited by to follow the discussion. A search result can be a thesis, preprint or review as well as a journal article.

Google Scholar's search help

Search Norwegian research with Sikt

Open NVA, the Norwegian Research Information Repository, at nva.sikt.no. Search a topic, title or researcher, for example Kristine Jørgensen. You can search without logging in.

Use the filters to narrow by institution or result type. To look for downloadable material, use the uploaded-file filter. Open a record and check whether a file is available; some records contain information without full text.

Check the author, year, journal and article type on the publication record. If peer review matters for your task, check the journal's policy and the type of contribution. A PDF or a search ranking does not establish this.

MAKE SOMETHING / INVESTIGATE SOMETHING

Building it and studying it can work together.

A game, app or company website can be both a practical project and something you study. “Game studies” is a research field. “Game development” is a making practice. An app is a type of product. None of these tells us the whole genre or purpose.

Game studies

Ask: how do rules, stories, players and communities make meaning?

Try: closely analyse one playable moment or observe how players understand it.

Search: game studies player agency, game aesthetics, Kristine Jørgensen game interfaces.

Game Studies: an academic journal

Game development

Build: mechanics, levels, dialogue, feedback or a working prototype.

Ask: does the design produce the experience you intended?

Try: change one mechanic, playtest both versions and compare what players do.

Search: game design playtesting, level design player guidance.

Connect your prototype to interface research

App and web development

Build: an app, website, interface or useful tool.

Ask: can the intended users complete the task, including people using assistive technology?

Try: watch someone attempt a realistic task. Check keyboard access, feedback and errors.

Search: human computer interaction usability, accessible interaction design.

Interactive work for a company or organisation

Ask: whose problem are you solving, and does the solution work for those people?

Search: participatory design, service design user research, design justice.

Combine reading lists. Make the connection precise.

CITATION BENCH / CHICAGO AUTHOR-DATE

A short citation in your text. The full article in your reference list.

Chicago author-date uses parentheses in the text and an alphabetical reference list. Cite paraphrased ideas too. Follow your assignment's instructions.

In your text

When referring to the article as a whole: (Jørgensen 2012).

For a passage you have read on page 142: (Jørgensen 2012, 142). Use the page you actually discuss. Quoted words also need quotation marks.

In your reference list

Jørgensen, Kristine. 2012. “Between the Game System and the Fictional World: A Study of Computer Game Interfaces.” Games and Culture 7 (2): 142–63. https://doi.org/10.1177/1555412012440315.

This is a real journal article. The citation demonstrates the format; it is not a quotation or a claim about a particular passage. Check the publisher's record.

Build the reference from your article's details

Pattern: Surname, First name. Year. “Article Title.” Journal Title volume (issue): full article page range or article ID. DOI link.

The article title uses quotation marks; the journal title is italic. Give the whole article's pages in the reference and the passage's page in the text. Prefer a DOI link when available.

In the text, name both authors for a two-author work. For three or more, use the first surname plus et al. Check the official guide for reference-list names.
